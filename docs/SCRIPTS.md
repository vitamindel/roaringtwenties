## Exhibit 1: The Aftermath of World War I

Save as **media/audio/room1.mp3**. Theme: *Why did Americans want a return to normalcy?*

Picture America in 1919.

Soldiers were returning home, but the country faced economic trouble, strikes, and fears of a communist revolution.

The Palmer Raids arrested thousands of suspected radicals, while Wilson’s plan to join the League of Nations failed.

Americans were exhausted by instability, so when Warren G. Harding promised a return to normalcy, voters embraced his message and gave him a landslide victory in 1920.

But did normalcy solve America’s problems, or simply promise relief?

*(76 words, about 28 seconds)*

## Exhibit 2: Inside Harding’s White House

Save as **media/audio/room2.mp3**. Theme: *What did normalcy mean for government and business?*

Harding’s return to normalcy meant supporting businesses and limiting government spending.

His administration introduced a federal budget system, cut taxes, and raised tariffs to protect American manufacturers.

Supporters believed these policies would restore prosperity, while critics argued the wealthy benefited the most.

Labor disputes revealed another side of his presidency.

During a major railroad strike in 1922, Harding’s administration obtained a sweeping court order against workers.

Normalcy was not simply doing less.

It meant making choices about who the government would help.

*(82 words, about 30 seconds)*

## Exhibit 3: The Teapot Dome Scandal

Save as **media/audio/room3.mp3**. Theme: *What happens when public office serves private interests?*

Grab a notebook, reporter. Something smells like oil.

Before the war, the government set aside oil fields as an emergency fuel supply for the Navy:

one at Elk Hills in California, and one under a rock formation in Wyoming called Teapot Dome.

In 1921, President Harding moved control of these reserves from the Navy to the Interior Department, run by Secretary Albert B. Fall.

Fall then leased the reserves to two oilmen, Harry Sinclair and Edward Doheny, secretly and without competitive bidding.

Around the same time, Fall suddenly had a lot of money.

In April 1922, the Wall Street Journal reported the Teapot Dome lease, and the Senate opened an investigation.

Senator Thomas Walsh asked the key question: how did Fall get rich so quickly?

The answer included a $100,000 “loan” from Doheny.

In 1929, Fall was convicted of accepting a bribe, the first former cabinet officer to go to prison.

Here is the careful part.

Harding died in 1923, before most of this came out, and there is no evidence he took oil bribes himself.

Good reporting separates proof from rumor.

So ask yourself: what safeguards should stop officials from using public resources for private gain?

*(197 words, about 72 seconds)*

## Exhibit 4: The Age of Calvin Coolidge

Save as **media/audio/room4.mp3**. Theme: *Was quiet government good government?*

After Harding’s death in 1923, Calvin Coolidge became president.

Known as “Silent Cal,” he supported lower taxes, balanced budgets, and limited federal government.

During his presidency, consumer goods like cars and radios became increasingly popular, and stock prices rose.

Supporters credited his policies for economic growth.

However, farmers continued struggling with low prices, and Coolidge vetoed bills designed to help them.

The decade brought prosperity for many, but not everyone benefited equally.

Was quiet government truly good?

*(77 words, about 28 seconds)*

## Exhibit 5: America and the World

Save as **media/audio/room5.mp3**. Theme: *Was America really turning inward?*

Although America rejected membership in the League of Nations, it remained involved in international affairs.

American banks helped stabilize Germany’s economy through the 1924 Dawes Plan, which reorganized reparations payments and arranged loans.

These loans helped Germany pay Britain and France, which then repaid their American war debts.

The United States also supported naval arms limitations and the Kellogg-Briand Pact, which renounced war.

America had not completely turned inward.

Instead, it chose which international responsibilities to accept.

*(77 words, about 28 seconds)*

## Exhibit 6: Normalcy for Whom?

Save as **media/audio/room6.mp3**. Theme: *Did everyone benefit from America’s return to normalcy?*

Normalcy did not benefit everyone equally.

Immigration laws in 1921 and 1924 sharply restricted newcomers, favoring northern and western Europeans while excluding almost all immigration from Asia.

Black Americans found new opportunities during the Great Migration, but also faced racial violence, including the Tulsa Race Massacre.

Meanwhile, a proposed federal anti-lynching bill failed in the Senate.

These experiences revealed that normalcy meant different things to different Americans.

Can a country truly return to normal if that normal excludes some of its people?

*(82 words, about 30 seconds)*
